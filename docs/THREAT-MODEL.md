# Declaration limitations and misuse

A declaration records a publisher’s claim. The project does not verify it, detect AI or provide certification.

## False or incomplete claims

A publisher may understate AI’s role, claim review that never happened, or describe a substantive AI contribution as decorative. Definitions, a surfaces breakdown and an explicit workflow make those claims easier to examine; they do not prevent deception.

Level 5 describes lack of pre-publication review. It does not transfer responsibility from the publisher to a model.

## Coercion and stigma

A client or platform may impose a misleading declaration or treat a number as a quality ranking. Neutral copy and equal visual treatment reduce signals of hierarchy, but cannot prevent coercion or protect employment rights.

An absent declaration establishes no workflow. It must not be used to accuse a publisher of concealed AI use or to infer human authorship.

## Incorrect mappings

A legal icon, IPTC source type or Schema.org extension may be misunderstood as approval or compliance. Keep proposed mappings explicit, use asset-specific interpretation and distinguish vocabulary support from platform features.

## Translation and classification drift

Automated checks enforce locale structure, placeholders, IDs and question branches. Semantic equivalence needs fluent human review. The known Level 3 review shortcut remains visible in both classification tools and project documentation.

## Artifact and supply-chain integrity

Static builds, schemas, dependency audits, pinned Actions, browser checks and a restrictive CSP protect the site and generated assets. A compromised repository or deployment can still substitute definitions or badges. Pin a release when reproducibility matters.

## Privacy

Inline and downloaded marks avoid requests to usagescale.org. Hosted assets reach the CDN and may enter operational logs. The site has no application analytics or signature registry. See [Privacy](PRIVACY.md) and [Security](../SECURITY.md).
